About us

It really is so much more than clothing

How a girl who once hid her culture started a nonprofit to share it.

Why I started Threads of Joy

If someone asked me when I feel the most beautiful, I would have a clear answer. It is when others in my community surround me, celebrating our culture, all dressed up in traditional outfits. I love the feeling of spinning in my sparkly lehenga and jingling as I dance to my favorite Bollywood music.

But if you asked me this question 5 years ago, I would have had a very different answer. Little 10-year-old Kavya had a very different idea of beautiful. One that definitely did not include embarrassing myself in my cultural clothing. At 7 years old, my family and I moved from New Delhi, India, to the United States. I was determined to erase every Indian part of myself to make me more American.

Read Kavya's full storyShow less

If I could talk to that little girl now, I would tell her that the US is an amazing place made of all kinds of cultures, religions, and nationalities. I didn't have to erase any part of myself to fit in, and the only way I would find my people was when I learned to embrace every part of myself.

As I started to wear traditional clothing more often, I discovered how amazing it felt. I felt a connection to my culture like never before and began to wonder how I hated this all these years. However, through this process, I discovered kids grow out of traditional Indian clothing incredibly fast. It seemed so wasteful, only wearing a piece of clothing once or twice a year before having to tuck it away in a closet for years, not knowing what to do with it.

This exact situation made my mind start racing. What else could be done with this clothing? I knew a ton of friends who dealt with the same problem and decided to research what people were doing with their gently used Indian clothing. I discovered an entire community of South Asian women with saris, kurtis, lehengas, and dupattas going unused after Diwali and weddings. That's when I started working on Threads of Joy. It started as a poster and logo on Canva, and snowballed into this amazing organization.

When I first started, I would have been overjoyed if even one girl could feel the way I did wearing the clothes I loved. Thank you to everyone who has donated, volunteered, partnered with us, or even just read this message all the way through! Your contribution is seen and so appreciated. It really is so much more than clothing.

Kavya Dutt BhardwajFounder, Threads of Joy

Why cultural clothing matters

The story stitched into a dupatta

Culture, renamed

Chai became the chai tea latte. The dupatta became the "Scandinavian scarf" on prom dresses, with no credit to where they came from.

A symbol of resistance

Sarojini Naidu championed khadi, the cloth used in dupattas, during the Swadeshi movement against British goods.

Clothing is dignity

Many families in Delhi can't afford new clothes for Diwali. A first lehenga that isn't torn can change how a girl feels at her temple.

Read the full essayShow less

Every day, I see more and more of my Indian culture being erased. The chai I would sit on the porch and sip with my grandparents in the afternoons has become the chai tea latte a commuter picks up from Starbucks late to work in the morning, no recognition of where it came from. The dupatta my mom carefully pinned to the shoulder of my lehenga has become the Scandinavian scarf, a new style on prom dresses. There is no recognition of where these things have come from and their importance to our culture.

For example, the dupatta is not just a piece of clothing. It is also a symbol of resistance for India during British rule. Sarojini Naidu, an Indian activist who worked alongside Mahatma Gandhi, was a huge activist for a type of fabric used in dupattas known as Khadi. It was part of the Swadeshi movement, which was focused on Indian national pride and boycotting British goods. When these traditions are stolen and made into something more digestible for global audiences, it makes us lose a part of our history and traditions. Even worse, young Indian kids grow up learning that version of our history, instead of where it really originated from.

Wearing cultural clothing and understanding the value and history that exist in the embroidery and style is the real mission of Threads of Joy. When kids have pride in what they wear, they will strive to understand the history behind it as well. It encourages education about lost traditions and connects new generations to those who came before. Many of the traditional pieces of clothing in my closet have been passed on by the women in my family for generations, and have sparked my interest in older styles of Indian clothing and how they came to be.

The lehenga hanging in the back of your closet or the folded-up sari in your drawer may seem useless to you, but for kids in underserved communities, it can be a dream come true. Many kids feel out of place during festivals and ceremonies without cultural clothing, since their families can't afford it. A common Diwali gift in India is new clothing, and many of these children's families can't afford it. This causes clothing insecurity and, oftentimes, a disconnect from culture.

Clothing insecurity is a phenomenon when someone does not have access to clothing that fits properly, is seasonally appropriate, or, in this case, is not appropriate for the occasion. Those who experience this can experience major tolls on their mental health and social dignity. Dignity is at the core of what we do at Threads of Joy, and the connection between clothing and dignity is concrete.

That dignity can exist in many ways, but the way in which we hope to reach people is in the light in a little girl's eyes as she holds her very first lehenga that isn't torn or broken. It's beautiful and almost brand new. That dignity exists in a teenager wearing her kurta at her local temple, finally feeling confident in her clothing. The judging stares she once felt on her back suddenly feel like radiant smiles encouraging her through every step. And most importantly, this dignity exists in a new generation growing up confident in their culture and fully aware of the stories that shaped it.

Every piece should be

  • Traditional Indian clothing for girls and young women
  • In a girls' size, age 1 to 18
  • Washed, with no odor or stains
  • Free of rips and tears
  • Working zippers, buttons, hooks and lacing

Please don't send

  • Torn, stained or heavily faded pieces
  • Broken zippers or missing closures
  • Everyday Western clothing
  • Clothing that hasn't been washed

Anything that doesn't meet these standards goes to Goodwill, so nothing goes to waste.

How to donate

Three simple steps

  1. At home

    Check and wash

    Go through the checklist above. Fold each piece and keep sets (top, bottom, dupatta) together in one bag if you can.

  2. Around Omaha

    Drop it off

    Bring it to one of our collection drives, or email us and we'll set up a drop-off or pickup.

  3. That's it

    We take it from here

    Volunteers sort, log and pack every item, then send it to our partner NGO in New Delhi.

FAQ

Questions about donating

Do the clothes need to be washed?

Yes. Please wash everything before donating. Pieces with odor or stains can't be sent.

What if my outfit is an adult size?

We focus on girls' sizes from 1 to 18. If you have adult saris or outfits you'd like to give, email us first and we'll let you know if we can use them.

Can I donate jewelry or bangles?

Right now we're focused on clothing only. We may add jewelry in a future drive.

Where do the clothes end up?

With girls in underserved communities in New Delhi, through our partner NGO there. It distributes clothing through schools, children's homes and community programs.

What happens to items you can't use?

Anything that doesn't meet our standards goes to Goodwill, so it still gets a second life.

Can I give money instead?

Yes. Shipping is our biggest cost. Travelers flying to Delhi carry our boxes as extra luggage, and about $60 to $100 covers one extra suitcase. Email us at threadsofjoyteam@gmail.com and we'll tell you how to give.
